on Charge-Steering Sampling

Abstract— We propose a charge-domain fractional- N all-
digital phase-locked loop (ADPLL) that employs charge-steering
sampling (CSS) of a sinusoidal reference waveform. The
well-known issue of 1 6quantization error in fractional- N oper-
ation is compensated by a capacitive digital-to-analog converter
(CDAC), which serves the conventional role of a digital-to-time
converter (DTC). This CDAC is further merged with the inherent
CDAC of a successive approximation register (SAR) analog-to-
digital converter (ADC), which is exclusively used for digitizing
the time-error mainly induced by the phase noise (PN). Initially,
the combined CDACs are preset to VDD, and then discharged
during a short digitally controlled oscillator (DCO)-divider-
triggered pulse via a pseudo-differential MOS pair directly driven
by the input reference sinusoidal waveform. Owing to the gentle
slope of the reference waveform, the charge-domain fractional- N
operation achieves a wide and linear time-error detection (TD)
range. Furthermore, by reinterpreting the SAR ADC output
using multi-bit midrise encoding, the effective time-to-digital
conversion (TDC) gain is boosted by bang–bang (BB) effects
while maintaining fast and robust locking. To accurately model
the CSS current, we introduce a damped-sine waveform model
incorporating harmonics, providing comprehensive insight into
the CSS-TD gain, even with short-channel devices. Fabricated
in 22 nm CMOS, the prototype achieves an rms jitter of 96 fs
at 24 GHz with a reference spur of −60 dBc in integer- N mode,
while 167.8 fs at approximately 24.5 GHz with a worst in-band
spur of −47.8 dBc in fractional- N mode. The occupied area is
only 0.08 mm 2.
Index Terms— All-digital phase-locked loop (ADPLL), capaci-
tive digital-to-analog converter (CDAC), charge-domain, charge-
steering sampling (CSS), fractional- N, low-jitter, mm-wave,
phase detection (PD), successive approximation register (SAR)-
analog-to-digital converter (ADC), time-error detection (TD).

With the introduction of digital-to-time converters (DTCs)

noise and integral nonlinearity (INL) of the DTC have become
the primary contributors to, respectively, the in-band PN and
fractional-N spurs [1] in fractional- N PLLs.
Several promising techniques have emerged for suppressing
the INL-induced DTC spurs: 1) approaches [29], [46] that
suppress the DTC nonlinearity at its source. For example, the
recently introduced differential DTC concept [47] minimizes
a sine-shaped INL by differentiating the large half-sine INLs
of two DTCs; 2) disrupting the periodicity of the DTC
nonlinearity in the loop through the use of higher order 1 6
modulation (DSM) [1], combined with dithering [32], [37],
[48], [49], [50], or employing a modified 1 6modulator
for the multi-modulus divider (MMDIV) [51]; and 3) digital
pre-distortion (DPD) methods to compensate for the DTC
nonlinearity [17], [34], [52], [53], [54].
The 1 6quantization error can also be compensated
using a digital-to-analog converter (DAC), a technique
dating back to 2004 with an IDAC cancellation in
conventional fractional- N charge-pump PLLs [55], [56].

DAC-based compensation has become increasingly pop-
ular [57], [58], [59], [60], [61]. In [57], a capacitive
DAC (CDAC) compensation is introduced in an analog
sampling PLL, exhibiting an elegant cancellation of the
1 6quantization. Both IDAC and CDAC compensations have
been applied to digital PLLs based on the reference-slope
sampling, as demonstrated in [58] and [59], respectively. It is
evident that CDACs in advanced CMOS [57], [58] offer supe-
rior linearity compared to conventional RC -based DTCs [24],
[62] in the context of 1 6quantization error compensation,
while also inherently supporting differential operation [58].
However, the key challenges lie not in the DAC itself but
in: 1) achieving a linear conversion from the phase error (or
time error) into the voltage (or charge) error domain and
2) attaining a high PD gain to suppress the in-band PN,
particularly quantization noise in digital PLLs.
A trade-off exists between the linear detection range and PD
gain. In [57], phase error detection is achieved by discharging
a capacitor with a “constant current source” during the interval
between the RC -shaped reference edge and the divided VCO
edge, functioning analogously to a charge pump. Its PD gain is
constrained by the constant current source as in a charge-pump
PLL [11], while the linearity is significantly limited by the
intrinsic nonlinearity of the RC -shaped reference edge. This
necessitates complex pre-distortion algorithms and circuitry
to improve the spur performance. 2 Similarly, the PD gain and
linearity are compromised in the voltage sampler for the sinu-
soidal reference waveform, as shown in [58], which requires
a Gm (thus, an additional nonlinearity) and a look-up table
(LUT) to mitigate the jitter caused by the analog-to-digital
converter (ADC) quantization noise and spurs induced by
higher order harmonics, respectively. For the oscillator-slope
(sub-)sampling with a high PD gain, as in [17], a digitally
controlled delay line (DCDL) was introduced to enhance the
linear detection range, while the CDAC compensates for the
residue of the 1 6quantization. However, that approach still
demands complex DPD algorithms and circuitry to address
the nonlinearity of the DCDL and the gain mismatch between
the DCDL in the time domain and the CDAC in the voltage
domain.
Recently, charge-sampling [63] and charge-steering sam-
pling (CSS) [41] techniques applied in the charge-domain
for, respectively, analog and digital PLLs, have demon-
strated very low jitter and reference spurs in the integer- N
mode. However, such superb noise/spur performance exploit-
ing the charge-domain operation has not been addressed in
fractional-N PLLs. In this article, we propose a charge-
domain fractional-N ADPLL based on the CSS technique [41],
[64]. Two preset capacitors are charge-steering sampled by
means of a pseudo-differential pair (“pseudo-diff-pair”) that
is directly driven by the sinusoidal reference and triggered
by a short pulse from the DCO divider. This approach
ensures a symmetric, wide, and linear time-error detection
(TD), (equivalent to PD in the phase domain) range for

By merging the differential preset capacitors with a fractional-
N CDAC and an successive approximation register (SAR)
ADC, we implement a fractional- N CSS-based ADPLL.
The fractional- N 1 6quantization error is thus readily
compensated in the charge domain by the CDAC, while the
SAR ADC exclusively digitizes the time-error caused by
the PN. To enhance the equivalent time-to-digital conversion
(TDC) resolution for low jitter, particularly under the slow
slope of the reference waveform, a midrise encoder (MRE)
scheme is employed, as opposed to sharpening the reference
slope as done in conventional sampling PLLs [24]. Further-
more, an analytical model for the fractional- N CSS operation
is developed, offering a comprehensive understanding of the
TD gain behavior.
The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section II
introduces the fundamental concept of fractional- N CSS and
its operating principles. Section III presents the proposed
charge-domain fractional- N ADPLL. It also includes the
quantitative analysis of the TD gain, featuring the proposed
model of time-damped sinusoidal waveform with harmonics
for the charge-steering current. Circuits implementations are
discussed in Section IV, and the corresponding measurement
results are provided in Section V.
II. B ASIC CONCEPT OF FRACTIONAL -N CSS
A. Prior-Art Charge-Steering-Sampling Technique
Fig. 1(a) illustrates the operational principle of the prior-art
CSS [41], which consists of two pairs of S 1 and S 2 switches,
a pseudo-diff-pair (i.e., M1/2) driven by the digitally controlled
oscillator (DCO)’s differential signal Vosc±, and an SAR ADC.
During the high-level of the reference clock ref, charge is
preset on the input capacitors of the SAR ADC through S 1.
Subsequently, a short pulse clock clk_css (triggered by the
falling edges of ref) initiates the CSS process via S 2 [see
Fig. 2(a)]. The net charge steered through M 1 (red shaded
area) or M 2 (blue shaded area) depends on the overlapping
area between clk_css and ( Vosc+ − Vosc−). The charge residue
on Csar (represented as 1Verr) corresponds to the time-error
(i.e., 1terr) between the reference pulse clk_css and oscillator
Vosc±. 1Verr is then digitalized by the SAR ADC to produce
the unsigned output Dout, which is subsequently processed by
a midtread encoder to generate the signed output Derr for the
ADPLL operation.

B. Proposed CSS of Sinusoidal Reference and Its Operating
Principles
To enable a true fractional- N operation entirely in the
charge-domain, we propose a new CSS technique shown in
Fig. 1(b). In addition to the two pairs of switches (S 1 and S2),
the pseudo-diff-pair, and the SAR ADC used in the original
CSS, a CDAC ( Cfrac) is introduced to compensate for the
fractional-N 1 6quantization error. Its role is similar to that of
the DTC [1]. This CDAC is further merged with the inherent
CDAC of the SAR ADC’s capacitor bank ( Csar) by sharing
the same top plates and reference voltage Vref, adc. Together,
they form the total sampling capacitance, Cs = Cfrac + Csar,
for the CSS operation. The basic operational principles of the
two CSS techniques for transferring the time-error (1t err) to
the charge domain (1 Verr on sampling capacitances Csar or
Cs) are similar. The key difference is that the original CSS
technique uses the reference clock ref to trigger the short
pulse clk_css to perform the charge-sampling of the sinusoidal
Vosc waveform, while the proposed CSS technique employs the
oscillator divided feedback clock (i.e., clk_div triggers clk_css)
to sample the sinusoidal Vref waveform.
Fig. 3 illustrates the complete operation of the proposed
fractional-N CSS technique. Cs is preset to VDD via S1 during
the high-level of clk _di v, as shown in Fig. 3(a). Afterward,
clk_css connects Csar and Cfrac to the pseudo-diff-pair via S 2
for the CSS operation, as shown in Fig. 3(b). The net over-
lapping area between clk_css and ( Vref+ − Vref−) in Fig. 2(b)
represents 1Verr for the given 1terr. Due to the relatively flat
slope of Vref±, the corresponding KTD is smaller but more
uniform compared to the prior-art CSS, yet its linear detection
range is expected to be significantly wider (e.g., ±Tref/8,
where Tref is the reference period). The wide linear detec-
tion range ensures flat KTD across 1terr variations, playing
a crucial role in linearly transferring the 1 6quantization
error, introduced by the DSM, from the time domain to the
charge domain. This error can be effectively compensated by
the linear CDAC at the subsequent falling edges of clk_frac
following clk_css, as illustrated in Fig. 3(c). Consequently, the
SAR ADC only digitizes 1Verr caused by PN [see Fig. 3(d)] at
the falling edges of clk_sar after clk_frac. Both clk_sar and
clk_frac are generated from clk_div using an inverter-based
delay chain.
III. C HARGE -DOMAIN FRACTIONAL -N
ADPLL B ASED ON CSS
A. Architecture
The overall architecture of the proposed charge-domain
fractional-N ADPLL is illustrated in Fig. 4. Its feedforward
path comprises the time-error detector based on CSS, merged
with the SAR ADC and the fractional- N CDAC, a digital
loop filter (DLF), and a DCO. The DLF is proceeded by
a MRE to improve the equivalent resolution of the TDC
functionality. Additionally, it comprises a digital dead-zone
(DZ) in the integral path to avoid any conflicts with the
proportional path [9], [41]. The feedback path is composed
of the high-speed current-mode logic (CML) divider, MMDIV
with the DSM, and other digital logic circuitry, enabling the
fractional-N operation. The normalized CDAC gain (Kdac,norm)
is self-calibrated by the LMS algorithm to minimize the
fractional-N spurs.
The CML divider (÷2) is included because the
MMDIV cannot directly handle the mmW DCO operating
at 21–25 GHz. To simplify the fractional- N analysis, we treat
the DCO and the CML divider as a single unit with an output
clock clk_osc having a period of Tosc for MMDIV , while the
intrinsic period of the resonating DCO is T ′osc = Tosc/2. The
time-averaged frequency division ratio of the MMDIV is N,
where Tref = N Tosc, comprising an integer part ( Nint) and a
fractional part ( Nfrac).

Robert Bogdan Staszewski (Fellow, IEEE) was
born in Bialystok, Poland. He received the B.Sc.
(summa cum laude), M.Sc., and Ph.D. degrees in
electrical engineering from The University of Texas
at Dallas, Richardson, TX, USA, in 1991, 1992, and
2002, respectively.
From 1991 to 1995, he was with Alcatel Net-
work Systems, Richardson, involved in SONET
cross-connect systems for fiber optics communi-
cations. He joined Texas Instruments Inc., Dallas,
TX, USA, in 1995, where he was an Elected Dis-
tinguished Member of Technical Staff (limited to 2% of technical staff).
From 1995 to 1999, he was engaged in advanced CMOS read channel
development for hard disk drives. In 1999, he co-started the Digital RF
Processor (DRP) Group, Texas Instruments, with a mission to invent new
digitally intensive approaches to traditional RF functions for integrated radios
in deeply scaled CMOS technology. He was appointed as a CTO of the
DRP Group, from 2007 to 2009. In 2009, he joined the Delft University
of Technology, Delft, The Netherlands, where he currently holds a guest
appointment as a Full Professor (Antoni van Leeuwenhoek Hoogleraar ). Since
2014, he has been a Full Professor at University College Dublin (UCD),
Dublin, Ireland. He is also a Guest Full Professor with AGH University
of Krakow, Krakow, Poland. In addition, he is a co-founder of a start-
up company, Equal1 Labs, with design centers located in Silicon Valley,
Dublin, Delft, and Timisoara, Romania, aiming to produce single-chip CMOS
quantum computers. He has authored or co-authored six books, 11 book
chapters, 180 journals, and 230 conference publications, and holds 220 issued
U.S. patents. His research interests include nanoscale CMOS architectures and
circuits for frequency synthesizers, transmitters and receivers, and quantum
computers.
Prof. Staszewski was a recipient of the 2012 IEEE Circuits and Systems
Industrial Pioneer Award. He also serves on the Technical Program Committee
of IEEE VLSI Symposium on Circuits and IEEE European Solid-State
Circuits Conference (ESSCIRC).
